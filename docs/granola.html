01 Requirements & Scope

Core features

  • ✓ Silent, bot-free meeting capture — no visible participant joins the call
  • ✓ Live rough-note-taking during the meeting, later blended with the transcript by an LLM
  • ✓ Cross-platform: macOS, Windows, iPhone (web app can view/edit but cannot capture audio)
  • ✓ Works with any meeting surface that plays audio through the computer — Zoom, Google Meet, Teams, Slack huddles, FaceTime, even in-person conversations
  • ✓ Reusable "Recipes" (prompt templates) for consistent, repeatable note formats per meeting type
  • ✓ MCP server exposing meeting context to external AI tools (Claude, ChatGPT, Cursor)

Functional requirements

  • Capture combined system audio + microphone locally, without joining as a visible bot or requiring per-platform API integration.
  • Auto-detect scheduled meetings via calendar sync (Google/Microsoft) and prompt the user roughly a minute before any call with 2+ attendees. (sourced: research summary of Granola's product behavior)
  • Transcribe in real time via third-party ASR, then combine that transcript with the user's own rough notes to generate a structured, personalized summary.
  • Support enterprise trust requirements: SOC 2 Type 2, SSO/SAML, org-wide data-retention controls, contractual AI-training opt-out. (sourced: Granola Security)

Non-functional requirements

  • Transcription must keep pace with live audio (real-time, not batch) since notes are being edited by the user during the call.
  • No persistent audio storage — a hard privacy constraint baked into the architecture, not just a policy.
  • Enhanced-note generation must complete quickly enough post-meeting that "notes are ready" feels near-instant, not a multi-minute wait. (inferred UX requirement — not an officially stated SLA)

Out of scope

  • ✕ Meeting-bot presence (the Otter/Fireflies/Fathom model of a visible AI participant) — Granola's entire pitch is the absence of this.
  • ✕ Video capture or storage — audio-only by design.
  • ✕ On-device/local transcription — Granola routes audio to cloud ASR providers rather than running local Whisper-style models. (sourced: multiple third-party comparisons vs. local-transcription alternatives)
💡 Interview Tip

Don't lead with "it transcribes meetings" — every competitor does that. The defensible product idea is that Granola treats the user's own typed notes as the anchor/outline and uses the transcript as supporting context, rather than generating notes purely from the transcript. That inverts the usual "AI summarizes, human edits" flow into "human frames, AI fills in" — and it's why the notes read like the user's own voice instead of a generic AI digest.

02 Capacity Estimation

Assumptions

  • 50M meetings/year (sourced) → ~137,000 meetings/day average
  • Average meeting length: 35 minutes (inferred — reasonable business-meeting average, not officially disclosed)
  • Meetings cluster inside a ~16-hour effective global window (accounting for overlapping business hours across timezones), with roughly 2× additional bunching at common start times (the top of each hour)
  • Continuous compressed audio stream at ~24 kbps per meeting (typical for real-time voice-optimized streaming codecs)
  • Average transcript ≈ 4,500 spoken words per 35-minute meeting, ≈ 27KB of text; enhanced notes add another ~2KB

Throughput / bandwidth (peak concurrency)

Total meeting-minutes/day = 137,000 meetings × 35 min = 4,795,000 minutes/day
Average concurrent streams (16h active window) = 4,795,000 ÷ (16 × 60) ≈ 4,995
Peak concurrent streams (× 2 for hourly start-time clustering) ≈ 10,000

Peak outbound audio bandwidth = 10,000 streams × 24 kbps ≈ 240 Mbps aggregate

Takeaway: Unlike a discrete-utterance dictation product, Granola's core load isn't request volume — it's concurrent long-lived streaming connections. ~10,000 simultaneous real-time transcription streams at peak is the number that actually drives capacity planning here, not a per-request QPS figure.

Daily data volume

Audio streamed (transient, never stored):
  137,000 meetings × 2,100s × 24,000 bps ÷ 8 ≈ 863 GB/day
Durable transcript + notes text:
  137,000 meetings × ~30KB ≈ 4.1 GB/day

Takeaway: Roughly 200× more data flows through the system as transient audio than is ever durably stored — consistent with Granola's own claim that "no audio file persists" after transcription. (sourced: Granola Security, Granola transcription docs)

Storage sizing

4.1 GB/day × 365 days × 3 years ≈ 4.5 TB of transcript + notes text
(illustrative baseline — actual volume is materially higher given ~10% WoW growth)

Takeaway: Even at multi-year scale, durable storage is a modest, cheap-to-serve text corpus — the hard engineering problems are real-time transcription/personalization quality and concurrent-connection handling, not storage capacity.

Read/write QPS

137,000 meetings/day ÷ 86,400s ≈ 1.6 note-finalization writes/sec average
× ~6 peak-to-average ratio (business-hours clustering) ≈ 10 peak writes/sec

Takeaway: Finalized-note write QPS is tiny — the real bottleneck, as shown above, is sustained concurrent streaming connections during the call, not the write path after it ends.

Why this shape?
  • Granola outsources both transcription and LLM inference to best-in-class third parties rather than self-hosting models — the company's own stated strategy is to focus engineering effort on the product/context layer, not model infrastructure. (sourced: Chris Pedregal interviews)
  • Splitting "live transcript stream" from "post-meeting note generation" lets the real-time path stay simple (just forward audio, display transcript) while the harder, quality-sensitive personalization work happens once, after the call, with the full transcript available.
  • Storing only text (never audio) in the durable store keeps the privacy story simple and the storage footprint tiny relative to the audio volume that passes through.

Request flow

  1. Calendar sync detects an upcoming meeting with 2+ attendees and prompts the user roughly a minute beforehand.
  2. On call start, the client captures system audio + microphone locally, applies on-device echo cancellation, and streams the combined audio to Deepgram/AssemblyAI.
  3. A live transcript streams back to the client in real time while the user types rough notes alongside it.
  4. On call end, the orchestrator combines the full transcript, the user's raw notes, and any active Recipe template, and routes the request to whichever of OpenAI/Anthropic/Google is selected for that generation step.
  5. The enhanced note is written to the AWS-hosted transcript/notes store (encrypted at rest and in transit) and synced back to all of the user's clients.
  6. Configured integrations (CRM, PM tools) and any MCP-connected external agent can then query or receive the finalized note.

04 APIs & Contracts

Granola does not appear to expose a conventional public REST API for meeting data (no public API docs were found in research); instead, its external-facing contract is an MCP server that lets AI agents (Claude, ChatGPT, Cursor, etc.) query meeting context directly — a notable design choice, exposing an agent-facing interface rather than a developer-facing REST API. (sourced: Granola's own MCP integration messaging; exact tool schema below is illustrative)

MCP tools (illustrative shape, exact schema not publicly confirmed)

  • search_meetings — find meetings by keyword, date range, or attendee
  • get_meeting_notes — fetch the enhanced notes + transcript for a specific meeting
  • list_action_items — retrieve action items extracted from a meeting

Internal client↔cloud contract (inferred)

  • POST /v1/sessions — register a detected/started meeting session, calendar metadata attached
  • WS /v1/sessions/{id}/audio — streaming audio channel to the transcription layer

🔑 Critical Design Detail

The Note entity deliberately keeps raw_user_notes and enhanced_notes as separate fields rather than overwriting the user's draft — this preserves the "human frames, AI fills in" product model end-to-end: the raw notes remain the source of intent even after the AI-enhanced version exists, which matters both for quality (regenerating with a different Recipe later) and for user trust (nothing the user typed is silently discarded).

06 Bot-Free System Audio Capture

System Audio Output
Microphone Input
On-Device Echo Cancellation
Combined Stream
Cloud ASR

Granola captures whatever audio plays through the computer's speakers/output plus the microphone — not a per-platform integration with Zoom's or Meet's API. This is why it works identically across Zoom, Google Meet, Teams, Slack huddles, FaceTime, and even in-person conversations picked up by the mic. (sourced: Granola transcription docs, product blog)

Documented limitation: because capture is at the OS audio-mixing layer, Granola cannot isolate one application's audio from another — background music or other concurrent system audio gets mixed into the transcript. (sourced: Granola docs)

Tradeoff

A per-platform bot/API integration (the Otter/Fireflies model) could cleanly isolate call audio and often gets richer platform metadata, but requires building and maintaining a separate integration per meeting tool — and, critically, puts a visible participant in the call. System-audio capture trades that cleanliness for universality and invisibility, accepting audio-isolation limitations as the cost.

07 The "Notes That Sound Like You" Personalization Engine ★ Key Page

Raw user notestyped live
Full meeting transcript
LLM: blend + structure
Enhanced Notes

(using active Recipe as formatting instructions)

  • The user's own rough, in-the-moment notes are the anchor; the transcript supplies supporting detail and correction — not the reverse. Even a few rough notes measurably improve output quality over transcript-only summarization. (sourced: Granola product blog / third-party analysis)
  • This is explicitly framed by the company as the differentiator against generic "summarize the transcript" tools: output is meant to read like the user wrote it from memory, not like a machine-generated digest. (sourced: Granola product blog)
Tradeoff

Transcript-only summarization (what most competitors do) requires zero effort from the user but produces generic, voice-less notes. Anchoring generation on the user's own notes requires the user to type something during the meeting, but yields output personalized enough that users describe it as "sounding like them" — a deliberate bet that a small amount of user effort buys a large quality/trust improvement.

08 Multi-Provider Transcription & LLM Routing ★ Key Page

  • Transcription: real-time ASR from Deepgram and AssemblyAI. (sourced: Granola Security: "best-in-class transcription providers (like Deepgram and Assembly)")
  • Note generation: dynamic routing across OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google models rather than a single foundation-model dependency or a self-trained model. (sourced: Granola Security page, corroborated by founder interviews)
  • Echo cancellation runs on-device, before audio ever leaves the client. (sourced: third-party architecture analysis, consistent with Granola's own capture description)
Tradeoff

This is the direct architectural opposite of a self-hosted-model strategy (compare Wispr Flow's fine-tuned Llama models on Baseten — see the closing comparison table). Outsourcing both transcription and LLM inference to multiple best-in-class vendors means Granola avoids the capital and engineering cost of GPU fleet management entirely, at the cost of being dependent on — and paying per-call margins to — four external subprocessors for the core product experience. Multi-provider routing on the LLM side does buy some resilience against any single vendor's outage or quality regression.

09 Speaker Diarization & Platform Limits

  • Speaker identification (who said what) is only available for Google Meet (via a browser extension, Mac/Windows) and Zoom (Mac only, via a settings toggle). (sourced: Granola transcription docs)
  • Everywhere else — Teams, Slack huddles, FaceTime, in-person conversations — transcripts default to generic "Me" / "Them" labels, with the AI attempting speaker differentiation from voice characteristics alone. (sourced: Granola transcription docs)
Tradeoff

True diarization requires either platform-specific integration (a browser extension, API access) or heavier on-device voice-fingerprinting — both of which cut against the "works everywhere, no per-platform setup" value proposition. Granola accepts degraded speaker labels on unsupported platforms rather than compromising universality.

11 Recipes: Templated Post-Processing Workflows

  • Recipes are saved, reusable prompt templates a user builds once and applies to every meeting of a given type (e.g., "sales call," "1:1," "sprint planning") rather than hand-crafting formatting instructions each time. (sourced: Granola blog / docs on Recipes)
  • Recipes default to private but can be shared org-wide, letting teams standardize note formats across meeting types. (sourced: Granola docs)
Tradeoff

A single, fixed note format (no templates) would be simpler to build and reason about, but meeting types genuinely need different structures (a sales call needs different notes than a sprint planning session) — Recipes push that complexity into user-configurable templates rather than trying to auto-detect meeting type and format server-side.

12 CRM/PM Integrations & Action-Item Sync

  • Native integrations push structured output into HubSpot, Attio, and Affinity (CRM) and Linear, Jira, and Asana (action items), plus a general Zapier connector for everything else. (sourced: research summary of Granola's integration surface)
  • This positions Granola as a source of structured, actionable output rather than a standalone notes archive — the value is realized when action items and call notes land where the rest of the team's workflow already lives.
Tradeoff

Building and maintaining native connectors per tool (vs. relying solely on Zapier/generic webhooks) costs more engineering effort per integration but gives a materially better, more reliable experience for the highest-value destinations (CRM records, PM tickets) than a generic automation layer would.

13 MCP: From Notetaker to Context Layer

  • Granola exposes meeting notes/context to external AI tools (Claude, ChatGPT, Cursor) via MCP, explicitly framed by the founder as evolving the product from "meeting notetaker" toward an "AI context layer for every worker." (sourced: Chris Pedregal interviews / press coverage)
  • This reframes the core data asset: transcripts and notes aren't just a personal archive, they're a queryable context source other agents can pull from.
Tradeoff

Opening meeting data to external agents via MCP raises the privacy/access-control stakes considerably compared to a closed, single-app notes product — it's a strategic bet that the value of being "the context layer" outweighs the increased surface area for data exposure, which is presumably why the enterprise trust controls (Section 14) exist as a first-class, not bolted-on, part of the architecture.

14 Privacy & Enterprise Trust Model

  • Subprocessors: Deepgram + AssemblyAI (transcription), OpenAI + Anthropic (note generation) — third parties are contractually barred from training on customer data. (sourced: Granola Security)
  • Granola's own model training: by default, Granola trains on anonymized customer data unless the user opts out in Settings; Enterprise accounts have this disabled by default. (sourced: Granola Security)
  • Storage: US-hosted AWS VPC, encrypted at rest and in transit, daily backups; no audio ever stored. (sourced: Granola Security)
  • Compliance: SOC 2 Type 2 (certified July 2025), GDPR Data Processing Agreement available. (sourced: Granola blog, Granola Security)
  • Enterprise controls: SSO/SAML, org-wide configurable auto-deletion retention windows, contractual AI-training opt-out. (sourced: Granola Security)
💡 Interview Tip

Notice the layered trust model: (1) third parties never train on data, full stop; (2) Granola itself trains on anonymized data by default for self-serve users, but that's opt-out-able; (3) Enterprise gets (2) disabled by default plus org-wide retention/SSO controls. That's a textbook example of designing one architecture that serves both a low-friction prosumer tier and a high-trust enterprise tier without a separate deployment — the same pattern shows up in Wispr Flow's Privacy Mode/Cloud Sync split.

Scaling & Caching

Cache hierarchy: Live transcript segments are naturally ephemeral/streamed rather than cached in the traditional sense; the more relevant caching layer is around calendar data (avoid re-fetching a user's full calendar on every check) and Recipe templates (frequently reused per-user config, cheap to cache). (inferred)

Hot-key mitigation: Not a hot-partition problem in the classic sense — load is naturally distributed across independent user sessions. The real concentration risk is temporal: the "top of the hour" meeting-start clustering identified in Section 02, which is a capacity/autoscaling concern for the streaming-connection layer rather than a data-partitioning one.

Sharding: Transcript/notes data would naturally shard by user_id/org_id, consistent with the access patterns in Section 05. (inferred)

Multi-region: Storage is confirmed as US-hosted; no public information indicates additional regions are currently used for data residency, though Enterprise customers with data-residency requirements would be a natural driver for adding them. (inferred beyond the confirmed US-hosting detail)

Top talking points

  • The core bet is "human frames, AI fills in" — raw user notes anchor generation, the transcript supports it, not the other way around.
  • Fully outsourced AI infrastructure (ASR + LLM, four subprocessors) is the opposite strategic bet from a self-hosted-model competitor like Wispr Flow.
  • System-audio capture (not per-platform bot/API integration) is what makes it meeting-tool-agnostic — and is also the source of its main technical limitation (can't isolate app-specific audio).
  • The real capacity bottleneck is concurrent long-lived streaming connections, not request QPS.
  • MCP exposure reframes the product from "notetaker" to "context layer" — a significant strategic expansion of what the stored data is for.
  • The trust model layers three tiers (no third-party training, ever → opt-out self-serve training → opt-out-by-default Enterprise) onto one shared architecture.

Quick Q&A

Why doesn't Granola join the call as a bot?

System-level audio capture avoids putting a visible/awkward participant in the meeting, at the cost of not being able to isolate call-specific audio from other system sounds.

How does it personalize notes instead of producing generic AI summaries?

It anchors generation on the user's own rough notes typed live, using the transcript as supporting context rather than the sole input.

What happens if one AI provider goes down?

Both the transcription layer (two providers) and the note-generation layer (three providers) are multi-vendor, which plausibly supports failover, though the exact routing logic isn't public.

Why build an MCP server instead of a public REST API?

It fits the company's stated ambition to be a context layer that other AI agents query, rather than a platform third-party developers build conventional integrations against.
